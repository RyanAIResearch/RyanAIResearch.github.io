OpenFold3 · NVIDIA H100 80GB · single GPU

Custom triangle kernels that train OpenFold3 faster, and learn the same thing.

StableFold replaces the triangle-attention and triangle-multiplicative path in OpenFold3. Measured against three production backends across the three crop sizes of the official training recipe, 400 steps each — and run twice over, once from the released of3-p2-155k weights and once from scratch. Twenty-four runs on one H100.

Training throughput

Wall-clock time for 400 steps

Each bar is one complete run_openfold train process, timed end to end with /usr/bin/time — startup, compile, training and validation included. Same seed, same data, same precision; only the kernel backend changes.

Initialisation

This switch drives every chart and table on the page. From released weights fine-tunes the converged of3-p2-155k checkpoint — the realistic case, and the sharp numerical regime where a differing kernel is most likely to show. From scratch trains a randomly initialised model — the mild regime, and the one where large gradients would expose a coarse error fastest. The comparison holding in both is the claim. StableFold is fastest at every crop in both matrices; the margin against cuEquivariance is +3.0 / +4.5 / +1.4% from released weights and +3.2 / +1.2 / +3.4% from scratch. Every pretrained figure is a harness-recorded wall_seconds line with an init_check receipt; in the scratch matrix three of twelve predate the unified harness and lost their status file, so their time is reconstructed from run-artifact timestamps (about ±80 s on a ~5000 s run).

Power

Board power draw

The harness samples the GPU with nvidia-smi every 10 seconds for the whole of each run. Below is that log plotted as recorded — every sample, no smoothing, no fitting. Hover a curve for the reading at that moment.

Each curve starts when its own process starts, so the horizontal extent is the run length — StableFold's line stops first. Draw swings by hundreds of watts inside a single training step, which is why the traces read as bands rather than lines; that texture is the measurement, not noise added in plotting.

Power is a wash against cuEquivariance. Mean draw is 221 vs 207 W at crop 384, 280 vs 279 W at 640, and 311 vs 314 W at 768 — no consistent winner. StableFold finishes sooner at every crop, but it does not get there by drawing less. Against DeepSpeed the gap is real (320 vs 280 W at 640, 361 vs 311 W at 768).

Numerical parity

The loss curves land on top of each other

A faster kernel is only useful if the model still trains the same way. All four backends ran from an identical seed and dataset order, so their loss trajectories should agree to within run-to-run noise. Hover a chart to read every backend at that step.

The gaps above are all between 0.008 and 0.011, roughly 0.6–0.8% of the loss itself, and final validation loss lands between 0.0685 and 0.0693 across every arm that completed. Three independent implementations trace one trajectory.

AQLab Triton diverges on the larger crops — but only from released weights. It went to NaN at step 29 on crop 640 and step 10 on crop 768, never recovered, and those two cells have no training time or loss curve to report. From scratch it completed every arm without trouble. That is the whole argument for keeping both conditions: the failure lives in the sharp numerical regime of a converged model, and testing only from scratch would have missed it. Even at crop 384, where it completes, its agreement with StableFold (0.031) is three times looser than cuEquivariance or DeepSpeed.

How far this evidence reaches. Every cell is a single run, so there are no error bars; the low-single-digit margins against cuEquivariance are within the range a repeat could move, and only the direction — StableFold ahead at all three crops, in both conditions — is firm. The 400-step window also sits inside the 1000-step learning-rate warmup, so peak learning rate is never reached. Repeats and a run past warmup are the next measurements.
